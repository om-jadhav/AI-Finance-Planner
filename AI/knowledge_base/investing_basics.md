# What Is Investing?

Investing means putting money into an asset today with the expectation
that it will grow in value or generate income over time, so you end up
with more than you started with. This is different from saving, where
money sits in a low-risk account (like a savings account or fixed
deposit) and grows slowly but predictably.

## Why invest instead of just saving?

Inflation reduces the purchasing power of money every year — something
that costs ₹100 today will typically cost more next year. If your money
sits in a savings account earning less than the inflation rate, it is
technically losing real value over time even though the number in your
account is going up. Investing in assets that historically grow faster
than inflation (like equity, mutual funds, or gold) is one way to try to
outpace this erosion.

## Risk and return are linked

As a general principle, investments that have historically offered higher
average returns (like individual stocks or small-cap mutual funds) also
tend to have higher volatility — meaning their value can swing up or down
more sharply in the short term. Investments with more stable, predictable
returns (like fixed deposits, PPF, or government bonds) usually offer
lower average returns in exchange for that stability. There is no
investment that offers both the highest returns and the lowest risk at
the same time — every investment decision involves trading off between
the two based on your own goals, timeline, and comfort with volatility.

## Time horizon matters

The amount of time before you need the money changes what kind of
investment makes sense. Money you'll need in the next 1-2 years is
generally kept in safer, more stable instruments, since there's little
time to recover from a downturn if the market drops right before you need
the money. Money you won't need for 10+ years can typically afford to be
invested in more volatile, higher-growth assets, since there's more time
for short-term swings to average out.

## Diversification

Diversification means spreading money across different types of
investments (asset classes) rather than putting everything into one. The
idea is that different asset classes don't always move in the same
direction at the same time — if stocks fall, gold or fixed income might
hold steady or even rise, cushioning the overall impact on your total
portfolio. This app's generated plans typically mix multiple asset
classes (equity, mutual funds, gold, fixed income) rather than
recommending just one, for this reason.

## Compounding

Compounding means earning returns not just on your original investment,
but also on the returns it already generated in previous periods. Over
long periods, this snowball effect is a major driver of growth — it's
part of why starting to invest earlier, even with smaller amounts, tends
to build a larger corpus than starting later with larger amounts, given
the same rate of return.
